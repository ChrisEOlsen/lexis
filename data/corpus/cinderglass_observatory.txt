The Cinderglass Observatory sits atop Mount Aiden Ridge, roughly forty kilometers from the coastal town of Port Nevin. It was constructed in 1962 under the direction of astronomer Dr. Priscilla Adair, who chose the site for its exceptionally dry air and near-total absence of light pollution at the time.

The observatory's primary instrument, a 2.4-meter reflecting telescope nicknamed "The Ember Eye," was installed in 1965 and remained the largest telescope in the region for almost three decades, until it was surpassed by the Halloway Array in 1994.

In March 1978, a junior researcher named Thaddeus Okafor detected an unusual periodic dimming pattern in a distant star later catalogued as HD-4471. Okafor's observations were initially dismissed by the observatory's senior staff as instrument error, but follow-up observations over the next eighteen months confirmed the pattern was real. The star was eventually recognized as one of the earliest documented candidates for what would later be classified as a contact binary system, and the discovery is still referred to informally among staff as "the Okafor Dimming."

The observatory nearly closed in 1988 after a regional funding review concluded that Cinderglass had become scientifically redundant compared to newer facilities. It was saved by a public campaign led by local schoolteacher Marion Delacroix, who organized a petition that gathered over eighteen thousand signatures within six weeks. The provincial government reversed its funding decision later that year, on the condition that Cinderglass shift its focus toward public education and open one weekend per month for guided visits.

Those public viewing nights began in 1989 and continue to this day, now drawing an average of four hundred visitors per session. The current director, Dr. Sunniva Kess, has held the position since 2011 and is the observatory's fourth director since its founding.

A fire in the equipment storage building in 2003 destroyed a significant portion of the observatory's early photographic plate archive, though the plates documenting the original Okafor Dimming observations survived because they had been transferred to the regional university archive two years earlier, in 2001, as part of an unrelated digitization project.

Cinderglass is scheduled to undergo a major instrumentation upgrade beginning next year, replacing the original Ember Eye mirror with a new segmented mirror system, the first such upgrade to the primary instrument since its installation in 1965.
